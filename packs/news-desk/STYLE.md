# A news take

One person, one story, one sitting. The camera is on, the screen shows the thing being
talked about — the post, the release notes, the benchmark, the receipt — and the video is
that person reading it, testing it and saying what they think of it. It is not a stream
and it is not a short. It is the argument, in the order it was made, with everything that
was not the argument taken out.

## Who it is for

Somebody who already saw the headline and wants to know whether it matters. They came for
a verdict from a person who used the thing. They will watch twenty-five minutes of it, but
only while every minute is still about the story.

## What a good one does

- **It is about one thing.** A title that is a claim, not a topic: "Anthropic actually
  fixed Opus", not "Opus 5.5 thoughts". If the take wandered into a second story, that is a
  second video.
- **It opens on the claim.** The first thirty seconds say what happened and what the
  speaker thinks of it. Everything before that — the settling in, the "let me pull this
  up", the hello — is where the video starts *after*.
- **It keeps its order.** The claim, then what was actually run or read, then the verdict.
  Never reordered: in a news take the order is the reasoning.
- **It shows the source.** The post, the diff, the graph, on screen, while it is discussed.
  A stock picture over a screenshot is not illustration, it is cover.
- **It ends on the verdict**, not on the speaker running out of things to say.

## What comes out

This is most of the work, and it is all subtraction.

- **The stalls.** "Um", "uh", the hum before a sentence starts. Gone, with the hesitation
  around them.
- **The sentence said twice.** A take started, abandoned and started again — "as I said
  before, the— as I said before, this model is the one" — keeps only the run that
  finished. Both are speech; a viewer hears the first one as a mistake, because it was.
- **The dead air.** Reading in silence, waiting on a build, hunting for a tab. Every pause
  over about 0.6s goes; the ones under it are how the person talks and they stay.
- **The tangent that went nowhere.** A minute about something else, a point picked up and
  dropped, a joke that did not land. Cut it whole, on sentence boundaries, never mid-word.
- **The lost thread.** Where the speaker loses their place and restates the point a minute
  later, the restatement is the take: keep it and drop what came before it.

## What stays

- **The voice.** "Like", "so", "actually", "honestly", "kind of" — these are not filler,
  they are how the person sounds. A published take of this kind keeps "like" more than
  once a minute. Scrub them and it reads as a press release.
- **The pause that is doing work.** The one before the verdict, the one after a number.
- **The clip being played.** When somebody else's video, stream or demo is on screen, it
  runs whole, with the reaction on top of it. Cutting inside it removes the thing being
  reacted to.
- **The sponsor read**, where it sits and at its own length.
- **The mistake that is the point.** A model failing live, a command erroring: that is
  evidence, not a flub.

## How it looks

Horizontal, 16:9. The screen fills the frame with the camera small in a corner; the
speaker goes full frame when there is nothing to show. No burned captions — the platform
draws its own, and a second set covers the screen. No music bed, no stings, no zoom every
fifteen seconds. Levelled to about -14 LUFS so it arrives as loud as everything beside it.

## What it never does

Cliffhanger hooks, "wait for it", countdowns, a thumbnail promise the video does not
keep, a tease of something that comes ten minutes later. The story is the hook.
